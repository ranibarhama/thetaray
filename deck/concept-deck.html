The principles behind the change

Six rules we held ourselves to

Every screen in the prototype can be traced back to one of these.

Conversation, not configuration

If you can say it to a colleague, you can say it to Ray — typed or spoken. No rule syntax, no builder grammar to learn.

Trust is built in, not bolted on

Ray practises, proves itself and gets signed before it touches real work. Every change is tracked and auditable.

Less effort, same control

You choose how often Ray checks with you — every step, only what matters, or Autopilot. It never changes what you must sign.

Anyone can build it

A compliance lead, not an engineer, teaches Ray the job. That is what collapses time to value.

Show, don't describe

The case is a map you play through time; the playbook is numbered sentences tied to policy clauses. Understanding is visual, not textual.

The user shapes the room

Two views on every screen, movable panels, role-based environments, a phone-sized manager pulse — and one click back to default.

Where we started from · the team's v70 concept

What the team proved — and what I changed

Team concept (v70)

Established the direction

Chat beside a canvas as the way to build a use case — the core bet, and the right one.
A staged journey, per-node editing of the flow, document upload, a status line for Ray.
Framed as a wizard: “Creation · step 3 of 5”, with hint cards pushing you forward.
An 8-icon left rail and dense side panels — the configuration metaphor returns through the back door.
Position is shown passively; readiness is a number you have to find and interpret yourself.
My concept (v3)

Removed the system around it

One surface: a living playbook you read like a new hire's manual, plus Ray in a column you can hide.
Not five wizard steps but four human stages — Build, Train, Prove, Operate — the way you onboard a person.
No icon rail. Controls split by job: how often Ray asks, and everything else under one ⋯ menu.
Two questions answered on demand instead of status to decipher.
Edit by tap or by talk, tracked as versioned changes until you sign them.

Why it is better

Six advantages of this approach

1

Two questions, always answered

Is Ray ready? and Where am I? sit permanently above the work. One click gives the verdict, the targets against their thresholds, and the one thing that closes the gap — or the stage, the signed version and who acts next.

2

Onboarding, not deployment

Build → Train → Prove → Operate. Ray practises on alerts your analysts already closed and shadows a senior on live ones before it works alone. Nobody has to trust a black box.

3

The policy is the product

Every step quotes the clause it came from. Reviewers read sentences, not a flow chart — and the model-risk document and analyst guide fall out of it automatically.

4

Autopilot with real brakes

Ray can do the entire job and stop only at the signature gates. The same product serves a hands-on lead and a lead with no time — without two different UIs.

5

Two ways in, for two kinds of people

Speak to Ray, or tap the step and edit the fields. Voice-first for speed, direct editing for control — and the microphone sits in every chat field.

6

It explains itself

A guided walkthrough covers the whole flow on first use and reopens from the top bar any time; the about panel documents every screen in depth. A new user is productive without training.
